**Technical Analysis Report: Root Causes and Variable Correlations of Machine Failures in Manufacturing Process**

**1. Overview**
This report investigates variable interactions and their relation to machine failure using the `IOT.csv` dataset. The objective is to provide explainable insights that an AI agent with LLM capabilities can use to infer root causes from custom queries.

---

**2. Variable-Wise Analysis**

**2.1 Air Temperature [K]**
- Range: 295.3K to 304.5K
- Mean: 300.00K
- Shows strong correlation with Process Temperature (r = 0.88)
- Weak correlation with failure modes, slight positive with HDF (r = 0.14)
- **Implication**: An increase in air temperature generally causes a corresponding rise in process temperature due to environmental heat transfer. When air temperature is high, it may reduce the differential between air and process temperatures, making it more likely for the system to meet the failure condition for Heat Dissipation Failure (HDF). For instance, if air temperature is 301.5K and process temperature is 310.1K, the difference is only 8.6K, which is right on the threshold. If the air temperature increases even slightly, this could lead to a failure if the rotational speed is also below 1380 rpm. Hence, close monitoring of ambient conditions is essential in avoiding this failure mode.

**2.2 Process Temperature [K]**
- Range: 305.7K to 313.8K
- Mean: 310.00K
- Strongly correlated with Air Temperature (r = 0.88)
- Slight positive correlation with Machine Failure (r = 0.04)
- **Implication**: Elevated process temperature is indicative of internal machine stress or insufficient cooling. If it rises significantly while the air temperature remains constant, this suggests inadequate heat dissipation. Furthermore, when this temperature approaches the upper bound of the dataset (e.g., >312K), it often coincides with machine strain or failure, especially if the rotational speed is low. This sets the stage for Heat Dissipation Failure (HDF). For example, a process temperature of 312K and air temperature of 304K gives a difference of only 8K, which falls below the critical 8.6K threshold.

**2.3 Rotational Speed [rpm]**
- Range: 1168 to 2886 rpm
- Mean: 1538.78 rpm
- Strong negative correlation with Torque (r = -0.88)
- Slight negative correlation with Machine Failure (r = -0.04)
- **Implication**: Rotational speed serves as a core indicator of machine activity and energy use. While high speeds themselves do not strongly correlate with failure, they interact critically with torque in determining system power. When rotational speed is high and torque is low, the machine generates insufficient power. This often falls below the required threshold for maintaining operation and leads to Power Failures (PWF). For example, with RPM = 2855 and Torque = 4.8 Nm, power output is around 1434W, significantly lower than the required minimum of 3500W. Therefore, a machine running at high speed must also maintain a proportional torque to avoid underpowered operation.

**2.4 Torque [Nm]**
- Range: 3.8 to 76.6 Nm
- Mean: 39.99 Nm
- Positively correlated with PWF (r = 0.08) and OSF (r = 0.18)
- **Implication**: Torque reflects the twisting force applied during production. If torque is too high, especially at lower RPMs, the machine can produce excessive power, leading to overheating or electrical stress. If paired with high tool wear, this can exceed the overload thresholds for the product type and trigger Overload Failures (OSF). On the other hand, low torque at high RPM fails to meet minimum power requirements, leading to Power Failure (PWF). This dual role makes torque a critical balancing factor in multiple failure conditions. For example, Torque = 70 Nm at 1300 rpm could easily push power beyond 9000W.

**2.5 Tool Wear [min]**
- Range: 0 to 253 min
- Mean: 108 min
- Correlation with Machine Failure (r = 0.11) and OSF (r = 0.16)
- **Implication**: Tool wear accumulates over time and directly affects production quality and safety. When wear exceeds certain thresholds (200-240 min), the likelihood of Tool Wear Failure (TWF) increases drastically. Additionally, high wear in combination with moderate or high torque increases the risk of OSF, especially for lower-quality products (e.g., Type L). For instance, a tool wear of 240 minutes with torque of 48 Nm results in a load of 11,520 minNm, which exceeds the OSF threshold for Type L (11,000 minNm).

**2.6 Type (L, M, H)**
- Used in determining OSF thresholds: L (11000), M (12000), H (13000)
- **Implication**: Product Type defines acceptable stress limits. Low-quality (L) types have stricter thresholds for OSF, meaning they are more prone to failure under identical torque and wear conditions compared to M or H types. Hence, the same operating conditions may cause failure in one type but not in another. This classification should be factored into any predictive analysis.

**3. Machine Failure Modes**

**3.1 Tool Wear Failure (TWF)**
- Trigger: Tool wear > 200-240 min
- Observations: Tool wear correlates (r = 0.12) with TWF; few instances show TWF active.
- **Implication**: This failure mode arises due to excessive degradation of the tool over time. Once tool wear surpasses approximately 200 to 240 minutes, the cutting or machining process becomes unstable, leading to performance issues or physical breakage. Although this condition is less frequent in the dataset, it is highly deterministic — that is, failure will occur once the threshold is reached, regardless of other parameters. This failure can be anticipated and avoided with routine tool inspection and timely replacement.

**3.2 Heat Dissipation Failure (HDF)**
- Condition: Temp diff < 8.6K and RPM < 1380
- Strong correlation with Machine Failure (r = 0.58)
- Correlates negatively with RPM (r = -0.12)
- **Implication**: This failure happens when the system is unable to effectively dissipate heat. Specifically, when the difference between the process and air temperatures is less than 8.6K and the rotational speed is under 1380 rpm, cooling becomes inefficient. These combined conditions signal inadequate airflow or stagnation in thermal conduction. An AI agent can flag situations where, for example, Process Temperature is 310K and Air Temperature is 302K (ΔT = 8K), especially if the RPM is at or below 1350. Preventive measures could include increasing fan speed, ambient cooling, or operating the system at a higher rotational speed.

**3.3 Power Failure (PWF)**
- Condition: Torque * RPM (converted to rad/s) ∈ [3500, 9000] W
- Triggered when out of range
- High speed & low torque causes power below range
- **Implication**: This failure mode is linked directly to energy inefficiencies. If the power output—calculated using the formula Torque × RPM (converted to angular velocity in radians per second)—falls outside the operational range of 3500W to 9000W, the machine enters a failure state. For example, a reading of RPM = 2855 and Torque = 4.8 Nm gives approximately 1434W, which is well below the threshold. This situation usually arises when the system is moving too fast with insufficient mechanical force being applied. The AI agent should be trained to compute power from torque and speed values and flag conditions where output is either too low or too high.

**3.4 Overload Failure (OSF)**
- Condition: Tool wear * Torque > threshold by Type
- Thresholds: L (11000), M (12000), H (13000)
- Correlation with Machine Failure (r = 0.53)
- **Implication**: OSF results from excessive mechanical stress that exceeds acceptable levels for a given product type. This is quantified by multiplying Tool Wear (in minutes) by Torque (in Nm). If the product exceeds the specific threshold for its quality class (Type L, M, H), it is at risk of structural or operational failure. For example, a Type L product with Tool Wear = 240 and Torque = 48 yields a load of 11,520 minNm, which is above the 11,000 threshold, leading to OSF. The AI agent should perform this computation and use type-based conditional logic to assess OSF risk dynamically.

**3.5 Random Failure (RNF)**
- Probability: 0.1% chance, no pattern
- Not correlated with any variable (r ~ 0)
- **Implication**: Random failures are stochastic and do not follow any identifiable pattern or correlation with other variables. These represent hardware glitches, material inconsistencies, or rare anomalies in the system. Since they occur with a low probability (0.1%), they cannot be predicted with certainty. The AI agent should recognize such events as exceptions and avoid falsely attributing them to deterministic causes.

**4. AI Agent Integration**
A language model-based AI agent can use the above findings to reason over queries. For example:

**Query**: "Show me which products have rotational speed above 2790 RPM"
**AI Insight**: "These products exhibit low torque (~4.8 Nm) and process temperatures around 308.6K. Their high RPM and low torque combination violates the power limits, causing Power Failure (PWF)."

The agent can deduce causality by referencing thresholds, correlations, and specific failure conditions.
